# How Treasure MathStorm Turned Math Drill Into Adventure

Treasure MathStorm! was released for DOS by The Learning Company (TLC) in 1992 and later on Mac and Windows CD-ROMs. It is a side-view snow adventure aimed at **ages 6 to 8**, although the box says 5 to 9. A villain's weather machine freezes a mountain, and the child climbs its **three levels** to return scattered treasures to a castle chest. To get there, the child nets wandering elves whose math questions pay coins and reveal hidden treasure, and earns **four climbing tools per level** in a themed math room (clocks, then a balance scale, then place-value crystals). Shops require **exact change**. After each climb the child collects a toy prize at home base, then climbs again at a higher rank. Difficulty came in **six levels that adjusted separately for each activity**, and there was no timer, no lives and no "game over." Most 1990s reviewers loved it, and MacUser ranked it among the best blends of game play and math drill. One guide dismissed it as "style over content." For your own game, the ideas to keep are these: each answer becomes something the child uses straight away, each skill lives in a place with a story reason to exist, difficulty adapts per skill, rewards stack up, and the peril stays comic. The flaws to fix are repetition, hard-to-see counting objects, backwards digit entry, unvoiced prompts and thin parent controls. Some details could not be confirmed, notably the exact number ranges per level, the in-game prices and the parent options.

## A storm froze the mountain, and the child thaws it one treasure at a time

The story is a thin frame, but it is well aimed. The **Master of Mischief**, the villain of all of TLC's "Treasure" games, **uses a weather machine to bury Treasure Mountain in snow and ice and scatter the treasures from the castle at its summit**. The player, a "Super Seeker," climbs the mountain, recovers the treasures and returns them to the castle chest so the mountain can thaw ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/TMS_VGA)). The opening does something clever: the storm breaks exactly the things the math will later fix. Clocks "go cuckoo," scales lose their balance, crystals crumble, a magic crown freezes and the castle bridge is destroyed ([K.K.Atlas review](https://kkatlas.wordpress.com/2015/01/27/game-review-treasure-mathstorm/); [TLC parents' guide](https://archive.org/details/childrens-educational-software)). Later, a timekeeper elf needs his storm-scrambled clocks reset and the cave elves have lost count of their crystals, so the math has a reason to exist inside the world ([Capital PC Monitor, 1997](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). Sources disagree about who owns the crown. One archive description says a king regains his power once all the treasure is back. A Mac walkthrough instead describes the crown melting while the villain sits in the castle ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/TMS_VGA); [Vizzed walkthrough](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)).

The cast is small, and each character has one job.

| Role | Character | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Guide | Chirp, a snow bird | Gives hints at the start of play and whenever the child runs out of coins, nets or snowballs, gets hit, hesitates on entering a math room, or pays the wrong amount ([in-game hint text](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual)) |
| Problem-givers | Wandering elves | Each elf caught with the net asks a math question. Elves carrying a scroll give the "special number" that uncovers treasure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!); [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)) |
| Room helpers | A timekeeper elf; a gold-room character called Equality (one guide calls it "Equity") | The timekeeper taps his foot while the child works and shakes his head sadly at a wrong answer. Equality tends a balance scale ([LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/); [High/Scope guide](https://archive.org/details/highscopebuyersg0000char)) |
| Merchants | A different shopkeeper on each level | Sells nets and climbing tools ([LP Archive, part 3](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2016/)) |
| Gatekeeper | A Yeti | Blocks the castle door until the child solves a number puzzle, then honks his nose and lets them in ([LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/)) |
| Mild hazard | "Snowbullies," floating snowballs | Appear at higher ranks, knock the hero down and steal coins ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!)) |

The 1994 CD box advertised "nine delightful speaking characters" who give feedback and hints ([1994 box scan](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER3.JPG)).

Two choices matter most for a parent-designer. First, a former TLC artist says the hero was **drawn in a snowsuit and scarf that hide identifying features, so any child can imagine being the hero**. The child also types their own name at the start ([former TLC artist's page](https://sk8nbike.com/Treasure_Mathstorm_01.htm); [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)). Second, **the villain is never fought**. In the Mac version's finale, after all the treasure is collected, the crown melts and the villain's snow machine breaks down. The mountain returns to spring or summer, and depending on the version the villain ends up frozen or blown over with snow ([Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)). One Let's Play author found that ending underwhelming and silent ([LP Archive, part 3](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2016/)). Wikipedia describes only the repeating climbs and no finale ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!)).

## Each climb runs one loop: catch, solve, spend, climb

The mountain is **three stacked levels, with a clubhouse at the bottom and a castle at the top**. Each level is a snowy slope seen from the side. It holds wandering elves, snowball piles, a store and one math building, and it ends at an obstacle. The child can pass the obstacle only with **four of that level's climbing tool**, earned in the level's math room or bought at the store ([Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual); [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!)).

| Stage | Featured math room | Tool earned (4 needed) | What the tool opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Time Igloo (clocks) | Ice picks | A cliff where a giant tree once stood |
| Level 2 | Gold Room (balance scale) | Catapult parts | A frozen fountain; the catapult flings the hero up to the next level |
| Level 3 | Crystal Cave (place-value counting) | Ladder parts | The Icy Gate, where the Yeti also sets a number-pattern puzzle |
| Summit | Castle | None | Pick the correct ladder, deposit the treasures in the chest, receive a prize |

*Sources: [Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual); [LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/); [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443).*

Problems come from two places. **Out on the slopes**, the child walks or skates (there is no jumping) and swings a net at elves, and each catch uses up one net ([LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual)). A correct answer pays coins: **10 cents per elf** according to a 1997 review ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). A scroll elf's answer is a special number instead. The child walks to a snowball pile, adds or removes snowballs until the pile matches that number, and presses "check." On the right pile the snowballs blink and reveal a wrapped treasure ([Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual); [LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/)). **Inside the buildings**, each correct answer earns one of the four tool parts, and after the fourth the room keeps paying coins ([Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)). The store asks for **exact change**. The child places coins on the counter and can break a larger coin if needed; a wrong amount brings a hint, and prices rise as the child ranks up ([Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual); [LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/)).

These pieces form **two small economies that lock together**. In the first, coins buy nets, nets catch elves, and elves pay coins and reveal treasure numbers. In the second, math rooms give tools and tools open gates. Because the math rooms keep paying coins after the tools are earned, a child who runs out of both nets and money is never truly stuck. The in-game hint for that exact situation sends them back to the rooms ([Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual)). The option to buy tools at the store gives the child a real choice: practise in the math room, or spend saved coins to skip a skill they find hard.

At the summit the child picks one of several ladders; at the difficulty one player reached, only one ladder was correct. The child then climbs to the chest and deposits the treasure, and an elf hands over a present. In the DOS version the present opens into a parachute that floats the hero back to the clubhouse, and the unwrapped prize drifts into the prize room ([LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/)). Prizes are **toys such as a flute or a toy train, one per completed climb**, so the shelf doubles as a count of ascents. Clicking a toy plays a short animation, and some pairs of toys interact ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!); [LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/)). The climb then repeats at a higher rank. Each new rank brings more treasures, harder questions, higher prices, and Snowbullies that grow faster and more numerous ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!); [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443)).

**There is no timer, no lives and no game over.** The only setback is a Snowbully hit, which costs 5 cents in one account and 10 cents in another ([LP Archive, part 3](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2016/); [Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). The controls ask little of small hands. On DOS, the arrow keys walk, down plus an arrow skates fast, the spacebar swings the net, and Enter checks piles and confirms answers ([ClassicReload](https://classicreload.com/treasure-mathstorm.html)). A panel of red on-screen buttons in the lower-left corner can run the whole game with a mouse ([LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/); [Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). Children sign in from a "Club Sign-In" list of saved names, and progress carries over between sessions ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). An elementary-school computer teacher found the screen "busy" but "not confusing, even for a first grader." The climbing-and-buying system confused her at first, but she reported that the kids "had no problem at all" ([Sarasota PC Monitor, 1994](https://archive.org/details/sarasota-technology-users-group-1994-03)).

## Six math skills, each tied to a place on the mountain

TLC aimed the game at the first two years of school. The front of the box says **"Ages 5–9,"** and the product chart on the back of the same box puts it in TLC's **"Ages 6–8" math slot**, above Math Rabbit and below OutNumbered! ([1994 box front](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER1.JPG); [1994 box back](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER3.JPG)). Reviewers usually said grades 1 to 3 ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)), and one school catalog stretched it to kindergarten through 4th grade ([Enhance catalog, 1994](https://archive.org/details/Enhance_Winter_1994)). TLC's own catalogs list counting money and making change, reading and setting clocks, mental math, inequalities and number patterns ([TLC Parent's Guide](https://archive.org/details/parents-guide-to-selecting-educational-software)). They specify the arithmetic as **addition, subtraction and multiplication**, together with equalities and inequalities ([TLC 1995–96 catalog](https://archive.org/details/the-learning-company-1995-96)). No source mentions division or fractions, which TLC saved for a later title, Treasure Galaxy!. The box advertised "two ways to play": drill-and-practice questions and problem-solving questions ([1994 box back](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER3.JPG)). A 1995 parent guidebook said TLC built the game to the new national math standards of the time ([Multimedia Home Companion](https://archive.org/details/multimediahomeco0000unse_n1l4)).

| Skill | Where it lives | What the child does | How it gets harder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adding, subtracting, early multiplying; mental math; word problems | Elves on every slope | Answers a caught elf's question; a scroll elf's answer sets the size of a snowball pile | Harder riddles at higher ranks |
| Telling time | Time Igloo (level 1) | Sets a digital clock to match an analog one, or reads the time; two tries per question | Easy mode numbers the minutes and highlights the hour in red; harder mode asks for the time 15 minutes earlier or later |
| Equality and inequality | Gold Room (level 2) | Drags gold weights onto the pans until the scale balances | Weights already on both sides, fewer weights to choose from, at most 3 per pan; a "Mystery Bag" of unknown weight |
| Place value | Crystal Cave (level 3) | Counts crystals grouped in ones, tens and hundreds and enters the total | More and bigger blocks of 10 and 100, such as "ten piles of 10" |
| Money | Store on every level | Pays the exact price in coins, breaking a coin when needed | Prices rise with rank |
| Number patterns | Yeti at the Icy Gate | Supplies the missing number in a sequence | Patterns get harder over time |

*Sources: [Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443); [High/Scope](https://archive.org/details/highscopebuyersg0000char); [ASCD Only the Best](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41155130_0696); [LP Archive, parts 1–3](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12); [Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual).*

Most answers are given by **moving things on screen rather than typing**: setting clock hands or digits, dragging weights, building piles, picking coins. That suits five-to-seven-year-olds who are not yet comfortable on a keyboard. One reviewer did find that **two-digit answers were entered ones digit first**, which felt backwards ([K.K.Atlas](https://kkatlas.wordpress.com/2015/01/27/game-review-treasure-mathstorm/)). Wrong-answer feedback often teaches rather than just buzzing. A wrong crystal count shows how many crystals sit in the ones and tens places ([LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/)). The clock allows two tries ([High/Scope](https://archive.org/details/highscopebuyersg0000char)), and a correct clock answer sends a magic spark from the elf's watch ([LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/)). The idea that runs through the whole game is that **an answer is never just "correct." It becomes a quantity the child acts on.** The number becomes a pile to build, and the coins become exact-change practice at the shop.

## Difficulty rose separately for each skill, and only where the child had succeeded

TLC advertised **six math levels that adjust automatically to the player** ([TLC 1995–96 catalog](https://archive.org/details/the-learning-company-1995-96)). The most revealing account of how this worked comes from a 1993 Naval Postgraduate School thesis on motivation in educational software. It observed that the program raised difficulty only in activities the child had already completed successfully, and left skipped activities alone. Over time the gap grew until the avoided activities felt easy by comparison, and the child was drawn back to them. The thesis also notes that the child chooses which activity to attempt on each level ([DTIC thesis ADA276250](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA276250)). This is a gentle mastery model: it balances practice across skills without ever forcing a child into one they dislike.

On top of the per-skill levels sits a **ramp from climb to climb**. A Mac walkthrough counted 3 treasures per level on early climbs and 4 on later ones, with Snowbullies and higher prices arriving as the child ranks up ([Vizzed](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105443); [LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/)). Sources describe the ranks in two different ways. The in-game text runs from "Super Seeker" through star ranks to "Champion Super Seeker," each step needing two more treasures ([Free Game Empire](https://www.freegameempire.com/games/Super-Solvers-Treasure-MathStorm/manual)). A 1997 review instead describes "trainee" at 0 points rising to "champion" at 450 points ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)). A status display showed tools collected, tools still needed and money on hand ([High/Scope](https://archive.org/details/highscopebuyersg0000char)).

Parent controls were **thin at best**. One 1995 desk reference says a Customization menu let parents reset the difficulty of each activity ([Dr. Mandell's Ultimate PC Desk Reference](https://archive.org/details/dr-mandells-ultimate-pc-desk-reference)). No other source confirms that menu. No source shows any way to turn skills on or off, lock a level, choose between drill and problem-solving for a particular child, or print a progress report. TLC did sell a School Edition and a five-copy lab pack (listed at $119) ([ASCD Only the Best](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41155130_0696); [Software Plus 1996](https://archive.org/details/software-plus-1996)), but no extra record-keeping in them is documented.

## Painted snow, a faceless hero and a catchy Bach melody

The screens run in a simple chain: an intro story, the title screen, Club Sign-In, the clubhouse and its prize room, then three mountain levels with doors into small single-screen rooms (store, Time Igloo, Gold Room, Crystal Cave). After that come the Icy Gate and its Yeti, the castle chest, and a parachute ride home ([LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/); [Retro Replay, re-hosted MobyGames captions](https://retro-replay.com/db/dos/treasure-mathstorm/)). The 1992 DOS game ran at **320×200 in 256 colours**, with 16-colour EGA and Tandy fallbacks. It needed only an 8088 processor and 640 KB of memory, and the installed game is under 1 MB ([Pixelated Arcade specs](https://pixelatedarcade.com/games/treasure-mathstorm/techspecs); [Internet Archive metadata](https://archive.org/metadata/TMS_VGA)).

Opinions of the art changed fast. In 1992 a buyer's guide praised its "attractive graphics" and "great sound" ([Game Players PC Entertainment](https://archive.org/details/game-players-pc-entertainment-vol.-5-no.-7-holiday-1992)), and in 1994 a teacher called it "so vibrant on the screen" ([Sarasota PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/sarasota-technology-users-group-1994-03)). By the mid-1990s CD-ROM titles had raised the bar. One guide then found the Treasure series "flat, fuzzy" and said MathStorm was "still stuck with its old-style graphics" ([Computer Museum Guide](https://archive.org/details/computermuseumguid00mira)). The artist drew both winter and summer versions of the mountain ([former TLC artist](https://sk8nbike.com/Treasure_Mathstorm_01.htm)). The frozen landscape reuses landmarks from the earlier Treasure Mountain!: the cliff stands where a giant tree used to be, and the catapult launches from a former fountain ([LP Archive, part 1](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2014/); [LP Archive, part 2](https://lparchive.org/Super-Solvers-Series/Update%2015/)). One real readability flaw is documented. On the Mac, **blue and purple crystals blend into the dark-blue cave background**, which makes the counting task harder than it should be. The later Windows version was more colourful and easier to read ([Vizzed Mac review](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105525); [Vizzed Windows review](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105991); [K.K.Atlas](https://kkatlas.wordpress.com/2015/01/27/game-review-treasure-mathstorm/)).

Sound is what players remember most. The DOS version supported AdLib, Sound Blaster, PC speaker and Tandy audio ([Pixelated Arcade](https://pixelatedarcade.com/games/treasure-mathstorm/techspecs)), and the main theme is built on **Bach's Invention No. 8** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!)). A fan soundtrack release credits the music to Alan Nu and the sound to Bret Menze. It says the score was written for FM synthesis in 1992, rearranged for General MIDI in 1993 and re-recorded as digital audio for the 1997 edition. Its track list shows **a separate music loop for every location**, instant right and wrong stingers, and **five escalating "reward" stingers for each activity room** ([xeen soundtrack collection](https://xeenmusic.bandcamp.com/album/treasure-mathstorm-soundtrack-collection)). Reviewers called the music "jovial and catchy to the extreme" ([K.K.Atlas](https://kkatlas.wordpress.com/2015/01/27/game-review-treasure-mathstorm/)) and "a perky little mountain tune" ([Dr. Mandell's](https://archive.org/details/dr-mandells-ultimate-pc-desk-reference)). Sound effects were sparse: a net swoosh, a bell, and the hero's "arrggh" when hit ([Vizzed Mac review](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105525)).

The original floppy release had **no recorded speech**, so the elves' problems appeared as text on screen and the 1992 game assumed the child could read them. TLC's 1994 catalog does not flag the game for digitized speech ([TLC parents' guide](https://archive.org/details/childrens-educational-software)). Voices came with the CD editions: the 1994 CD box advertises speaking characters ([1994 box back](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER3.JPG)). A review of a later Mac CD describes elves reading each station's directions aloud until the child clicks to begin ([SUNY Potsdam review](https://people.potsdam.edu/betrusak/reviews/readerrabbittreasuremath.html)). One modern reviewer found that voice work "scratchy" ([Vizzed Windows review](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105991)).

## Reviewers praised the blend of game and drill, with one sharp dissent

Praise at the time was broad. A 1992 buyer's guide singled out its "charming little touches" for younger kids ([Game Players PC Entertainment](https://archive.org/details/game-players-pc-entertainment-vol.-5-no.-7-holiday-1992)). The 1993 Oppenheim Toy Portfolio said "it works!" and recommended it for ages 6 to 8 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!)). Technology & Learning's evaluators said it teaches what kids need "in the context of a game," as quoted by ASCD ([ASCD Only the Best](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41155130_0696)). MacUser's December 1994 roundup listed it at $42 and called it **"one of the best integrations of game play and math drill"** ([MacUser](https://archive.org/details/eu_Macuser-1994-12_OCR)). A teacher wrote that children were so absorbed they didn't notice they were "working hard" ([Sarasota PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/sarasota-technology-users-group-1994-03)).

Later recognition followed:

| Source | Year | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| ASCD, *Only the Best* | From 1996; "Classic" in 1999–2000 | Included; rated "Excellent" by Children's Software Revue ([ASCD](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41155130_0696)) |
| Score/Newsweek guide | 1997 | Editors' Choice, pitched at a child with "a serious video-game habit" ([Score/Newsweek guide](https://archive.org/details/scorenewsweekfam0000scor)) |
| Capital PC Monitor | 1997 | The reviewer's 6-year-old's favourite educational game ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)) |
| TLC's own promotional guide | 1993–94 | Claims a CES Innovations '93 honour and a Technology & Learning Award of Excellence; no independent source confirms either ([TLC parents' guide](https://archive.org/details/childrens-educational-software)) |

The dissent is worth taking seriously, because it names the exact risk your game faces. A 1996 book ranked MathStorm and Treasure Mountain! among the 25 worst programs as "style over content." It judged them passable as arcade games, but said that as education "they grade 'F'" ([The 18 Best (and 25 Worst)](https://archive.org/details/18bestand25worst0000dani)). The Computer Museum guide reported parents saying the math "doesn't always stick." It also described a 5-year-old who stopped playing the earlier Treasure Mountain! as soon as she had "unfrozen" the mountain ([Computer Museum Guide](https://archive.org/details/computermuseumguid00mira)). Even a guide that rated the game highly said it "sprinkles in some math problems" ([High/Scope](https://archive.org/details/highscopebuyersg0000char)). The common thread is **math density**: walking, netting and dodging take up a lot of time compared with the number of problems solved.

Later scores stay warm: 84/100 from HonestGamers in 2003 ([HonestGamers](http://www.honestgamers.com/989/pc/super-solvers-treasure-mathstorm/review.html)), 4/5 from K.K.Atlas in 2015 ([K.K.Atlas](https://kkatlas.wordpress.com/2015/01/27/game-review-treasure-mathstorm/)), and 4.4/5 from 172 MyAbandonware user votes ([MyAbandonware](https://www.myabandonware.com/game/treasure-mathstorm-1r6)). Adults who played it as children remember school computer labs, the music and swinging the net. One blogger recalls the Snowbully sound triggering "fight or flight," and that turning to net one felt cathartic ([Accordion Sprout](https://www.accordionsprout.com/blog/495-words-about-remembering-super-seekers-treasure-mathstorm)).

The same players keep coming back to a handful of complaints. The biggest is **repetition**, because finishing takes many climbs ([HonestGamers](http://www.honestgamers.com/989/pc/super-solvers-treasure-mathstorm/review.html)). Others recur too: **crystals that are hard to count**, **two-digit answers entered ones digit first**, a **flat ending**, and **Snowbully movements that are hard to predict** ([Vizzed Mac review](https://www.vizzed.com/boards/thread.php?id=105525)). Today, players also report **saves that break on modern systems** ([MyAbandonware](https://www.myabandonware.com/game/treasure-mathstorm-1r6)).

The price fell across the decade: about $40 in 1993 ([Fortune](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40706948_0103)), $29.95 for the CD in 1997 ([Capital PC Monitor](https://archive.org/details/capital-pc-user-group-1997-12)), and $14.75 for a Mac CD in 1998 ([SUNY Potsdam review](https://people.potsdam.edu/betrusak/reviews/readerrabbittreasuremath.html)).

MathStorm is the **second of four "Treasure" games**: Treasure Mountain! (1990), MathStorm (1992), Treasure Cove! (1992) and Treasure Galaxy! (1994). The four form a younger-child branch of TLC's Super Solvers family, and they share the Master of Mischief as villain and a "Super Seeker" as hero ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_MathStorm!); [Wikipedia: Treasure Mountain!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Mountain!); [Wikipedia: Treasure Galaxy!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Galaxy!)). Treasure Mountain! used the same three-level, elf-netting frame, but its riddles mixed reading, math and thinking. A retrospective reviewer summed MathStorm up as that game's "english and trivia questions" turned into math games ([HonestGamers](http://www.honestgamers.com/989/pc/super-solvers-treasure-mathstorm/review.html)). Treasure Cove! moved underwater for reading and science, and Treasure Galaxy! went to space for measurement, fractions, shapes and calendars ([Wikipedia: Treasure Cove!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Cove!); [TLC 1995–96 catalog](https://archive.org/details/the-learning-company-1995-96)). In TLC's math line, MathStorm (ages 6–8) came before OutNumbered! (8–10) and Operation Neptune (10 and up) ([1994 box back](https://archive.org/download/treasure-mathstorm-1994/COVER3.JPG)).

The company changed hands repeatedly ([Wikipedia: The Learning Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Learning_Company)). TLC was founded in 1980 and went public in April 1992, the year the game launched. In December 1995 SoftKey took it over for **$606 million** in a hostile deal. Mattel bought it in 1999 and sold it to Gores in 2000. The education titles went to Riverdeep in 2001, and the brand ended up with Houghton Mifflin Harcourt (HMH) by 2007. HMH is the likely rights holder, but no source confirms that for this title. **There is no official modern re-release.** A Steam search returns nothing ([Steam search](https://store.steampowered.com/api/storesearch/?term=mathstorm&l=english&cc=US)), and on GOG the game exists only as a "Dreamlist" wish, with 198 votes as of November 2026 ([GOG Dreamlist](https://www.gog.com/dreamlist/game/treasure-mathstorm-1992)).

## Design takeaways for an original game

Everything below is a **genre-level mechanic**, a structure many games share. None of it is a recommendation to recreate Treasure MathStorm. Use your own name, world, characters, art, music and writing throughout. The last subsection lists what to leave behind.

### Reuse the skeleton: a home base, three themed zones and a gate at the end of each

The structure that worked starts from **a cosy home base** that displays everything the child has earned. From there the child climbs **three zones in order**, each built around one featured skill station. Each zone has **a short, countable goal**: collect four pieces of the key to the next zone. At the top comes **a payoff**: the child deposits the treasure, receives a prize, returns home, and climbs again at a higher rank. Climbing turns an abstract score into visible, physical progress, and each zone gets a clear identity from its skill. The most underrated trick is narrative. In the original, the villain's storm broke exactly what the math repairs. Have your opening disaster break the specific things your child will fix, so each problem feels like helping someone rather than taking a quiz.

### Turn every answer into something the child uses next

This is the single most transferable idea. **An answer should become a resource or an action**, not just a tick and a sound: a number of things to place, a quantity to build, coins to spend with exact change. It is also the best defence against the "style over content" critique. Make the satisfying action itself mathematical, such as building the pile, counting out the payment or balancing the scale. Keep the walking between problems short, so the number of real problems per minute stays high. Let the shop be a math activity in disguise rather than a menu.

### Cover the original's six skills, then fit them to your child

The original's skill mix still suits ages 6 to 8, as long as you invent your own stations for it.

| Skill | Genre-level station pattern | Make it yours |
|---|---|---|
| Number facts (+ and −, then early ×) and word problems | Roaming characters who ask a question when caught; the answer feeds a second task | Your own creatures and your own catching action, with every question voiced |
| Telling time | Setting one clock to match another, then working out "earlier" and "later" | A clock that belongs in your world: a sundial, a train timetable, a bedtime routine |
| Comparing and equality | A two-sided balance, with an "unknown weight" puzzle later | Weigh something your child cares about |
| Place value | Counting objects grouped in ones, tens and hundreds | **Clearly different shapes and colours** for each group size |
| Money | A shop that needs the exact amount | Your own currency; the UK edition switched to pence |
| Patterns | A gatekeeper who asks for the missing number in a sequence | Have the right answer open something the child can see |

Aim the base content at ages 6 to 8. Use voice prompts and visual counting aids to bring in a 5-year-old, and add stretch levels for a 9-year-old. Save division and fractions for a later expansion; TLC itself put fractions in a separate, later game.

### Adapt each skill separately, and let skipped skills become inviting

Give **each skill its own difficulty track**, as the original did with its six levels. **Move a skill up only after the child succeeds at it**, and leave skipped skills where they are. Over time the skipped skills feel easy by comparison, and the child drifts back to them unforced, exactly as the 1993 thesis described. Let the child choose which station to visit and when.

Then add what the original lacked or never documented. Step a skill down gently after repeated misses. Give two tries, then a worked hint that teaches: show the tens and ones, number the clock face, or tip the scale to show the heavier side. Build a hidden parent area behind an adult check, where you can switch skills on or off, cap number ranges, start the child at a chosen level, and see accuracy per skill. Because the game is for one child, you can match it to whatever their class is covering this month. Keep the earn-it-or-buy-it option for gate pieces: it lets a frustrated child skip a skill today, paid for with coins earned by practice elsewhere.

### Stack rewards that pay off in seconds, minutes, sessions and weeks

The original layered its rewards well, and the layers are worth copying.

| Timescale | Reward |
|---|---|
| Seconds | An instant right or wrong sound and a small visual flourish |
| Minutes | Coins and key pieces, with a jingle that **steps up through five levels** during a streak |
| Each climb | Depositing the treasure and receiving a new collectible |
| Weeks | A growing shelf of collectibles that animate and interact when tapped, plus a rank title |

Show the child what they found instead of keeping it wrapped, since players mocked the original's hidden treasures. Two things guard against a child quitting once the main goal is done, as the Computer Museum guide's 5-year-old did. The first is a **real, celebratory, voiced ending**. The second is something to do afterwards, such as new collectibles, harder "expert" climbs or a home base the child can decorate.

### Keep the stakes comic and the villain off-stage

The original's tone suits young children: **no timer, no lives, no game over**, and a silly enemy whose worst act is knocking the hero over and taking a coin. The villain loses because of the child's good deeds, not because of a fight, and characters react with gentle comedy. Keep all of that. Do make hazards easy to read and adjustable, though. Players found the original's enemies hard to predict, and one adult still remembers the jolt of fear they caused. A parent setting that slows hazards or turns them off costs little. The original's faceless hero let any child imagine being the hero. You can go further and let your child design the hero.

### Fix what frustrated players the first time

| Known weakness | Evidence | Fix in your game |
|---|---|---|
| The same climb repeated many times | Later reviewers | Vary each run: shuffle station layouts, rotate in new mini-activities, unlock a new zone or season every few climbs, and keep each climb short |
| Crystals hard to see | Several reviewers of the Mac version | High-contrast art; shapes as well as colours for ones, tens and hundreds; tens shown in frames of ten |
| Ones digit entered first | K.K.Atlas | Left-to-right entry, the way the child writes numbers, or tapping labelled tens and ones blocks |
| Text-only prompts in 1992; scratchy voices later | TLC catalog; Vizzed | A clear recorded voice for every prompt, with a replay button and on-screen text |
| Few or no parent settings and no reports | No source shows any | A parent area with skill switches, number ranges and a per-skill progress view |
| A flat ending | Let's Play; HonestGamers | A payoff with music, voice and a visibly changed world, then a reason to keep playing |
| "Math doesn't stick" and "style over content" | 1990s guides | More problems per minute, math built into the actions, short walks |
| Fragile saves | MyAbandonware comments | Automatic saving with a backup, and touch and tablet support from day one |

### Make it yours: what not to borrow

Leave behind the original's names: Super Seeker, Master of Mischief, Chirp, Snowbullies, Equality, Treasure Mountain, the Time Igloo, the Gold Room and the Crystal Cave. Leave behind its character designs, art, writing, hint text and music too. Skip Bach's Invention No. 8 as your theme; it is so closely tied to this game that it would make yours feel like a copy. Avoid recreating its particular mix of a snowy mountain, net-caught elves, snowball piles and a Yeti gate. Borrow the structure, not the expression. Start from a world your child already loves and ask what broken things the story could give them to fix. One placeholder example: a sky garden where a prankish wind scattered the seeds and jammed the sundial, the seed scale and the market stall, and the child repairs them zone by zone.

## Conclusion

Treasure MathStorm's lasting idea was not the snow or the elves. It was that **every skill had a job in the story, and every answer became a tool the child used straight away**, while difficulty rose quietly and only where the child was already succeeding. Its critics and its fans describe the same tension from opposite sides. Children remember the music, the net and the comic danger, while some educators doubted the math stuck. A new game resolves that by making the satisfying action the math itself, rather than a pause between problems.

A game built for one child also has advantages TLC never had. The missing per-level number ranges matter less than they seem, because you will set them to your child's current schoolwork and adjust them week by week. The original's biggest gaps were spoken prompts for pre-readers, readable art, a parent view and an ending that rewards the effort. Those are exactly the parts a parent building a game is best placed to get right.
